Related works. The small-Weber-number shape of the bubble, an oblate spheroid with aspect ratio \(\chi = 1 + \tfrac{9}{32}\,\mathrm{We} + \dots\), goes back to Moore {{cite:moore1959}}. Moore's spheroidal model {{cite:moore1965}} and El Sawi's virial approximation {{cite:elsawi}} predicted that the Weber number has a maximum. Miksis, Vanden-Broeck and Keller computed the branch numerically {{cite:mvk}}: the bubble becomes oblate, the Weber number passes a maximum, and then the poles are pushed in until they touch; Shankar recomputed the branch over its whole range {{cite:shankar2005}}. Both expected that beyond the touching the bubble becomes a torus {{cite:mvk,shankar2005}}, which is what we find. Benjamin gave a Hamiltonian theory of moving bubbles, with variational characterisations of the steady ones and an account of multiply connected bubbles {{cite:benjamin}}. In two dimensions a bubble in a uniform stream likewise flattens until opposite sides touch {{cite:vbk1980,shankar1992}}. For rings, the speed of a thin hollow vortex ring goes back to Hicks {{cite:hicks}}, as Kelvin's formula {{cite:kelvin}} does for a ring with a uniform core, and Moore and Saffman derived how surface tension changes it {{cite:mooresaffman}}. Toroidal bubbles with circulation were treated variationally by Ruban and Rasmussen {{cite:rubanrasmussen}}, and rising bubble rings in a viscous liquid by Pedley {{cite:pedley1968}} and by Cheng, Lou and Lim {{cite:chenglouLim}}. On the rigorous side, besides the constructions {{cite:mns}} near the sphere and {{cite:ms}} for thin rings, Han and Seis proved that thick bubble rings cannot exist at low Weber numbers {{cite:hanseis}}.
